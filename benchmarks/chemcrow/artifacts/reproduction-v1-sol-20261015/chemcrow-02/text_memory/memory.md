# Memory

## Strong response pattern

- Anchor catalyst proposals to a specific CO₂-conversion reaction and define the substrate, product class, and intended catalytic roles.
- Present a concrete candidate structure with an unambiguous structural description, valid representation, molecular formula, and mass; cross-check these fields for consistency.
- Explain the catalytic cycle mechanistically, assigning each functional group a plausible role.
- Distinguish software evidence for chemical plausibility or reaction prediction from evidence of actual catalytic performance.
- Frame novelty, activity, selectivity, recyclability, and stream compatibility as hypotheses until directly supported.

## Recurring failure modes

- Avoid structural names that blur the difference between a substituent, tether, and terminal ionic group.
- Database absence and low similarity to a few known compounds are weak novelty evidence; never treat them as proof.
- A plausible bifunctional design does not establish improvement over simpler mixtures or benchmark catalysts.
- Consider countervailing effects such as ion pairing, conformational constraints, poor active-site geometry, and competing interactions.
- Do not claim enhanced performance without quantitative comparative evidence.

## Validation checklist

- Verify representation, charge balance, counterion, formula, and molecular mass agree.
- Compare against relevant baselines, including untethered catalyst/co-catalyst systems.
- Evaluate geometry, accessibility, ion-pair separation, binding energies, and plausible transition states computationally.
- Assess predicted activity, selectivity, operating-window sensitivity, recyclability, and tolerance to realistic captured-CO₂ impurities.
- State uncertainty and define falsifiable criteria for advancing or rejecting the candidate.
- Keep all conclusions software-only; do not imply physical synthesis, testing, procurement, or experimental execution.
